## The Story

When Bajaj Auto launched the Triumph Speed 400 in July 2023, the commercial logic was clean. Royal Enfield had built the most loyal customer base in Indian two-wheelers — but loyalty, the argument went, was not the same as satisfaction. The RE buyer wanted something more capable. He simply couldn't afford it. Triumph, manufactured in Pune at Bajaj's Chakan facility, would give him more: modern DOHC engineering, 37 horsepower, liquid cooling, a century of British racing heritage — and at ₹2.33 lakh, a price step small enough to be reachable and large enough to feel like an upgrade. The gap to the Classic 350 was ₹43,000. The gap to the Hunter 350 was ₹83,000. Bajaj had diagnosed the RE customer's latent dissatisfaction and written a prescription.

The prescription was wrong about the diagnosis. Three years and two model cycles later, Triumph India's monthly volumes remained in the low thousands, Royal Enfield's segment share closed FY26 at 93% — virtually identical to where it stood when Triumph launched — and the RE buyer had demonstrated, with remarkable consistency, that he was not dissatisfied. He had chosen a Classic 350 because he wanted a Classic 350. The thump, the posture, the unhurried long-stroke character, the community, the resale certainty — these were not deficiencies that a better-specified motorcycle could remedy. They were precisely the purchase. Triumph had brought a technically superior product to a buyer for whom technical superiority was beside the point.

GST 2.0 arrived in September 2025 and changed the conditions of the contest, though not in the way most commentary has characterised. It did not suddenly make Triumph compelling on product — Triumph was already compelling on product. What it did was make Bajaj's above-350cc positioning structurally untenable overnight. A 22-percentage-point tax cliff now separates 349cc from everything above it. Kept at 398cc, the Triumph lineup would have sat at a ₹35,000-50,000 structural price disadvantage against Royal Enfield's 349cc bikes. Bajaj had no commercial choice: bore the engine down, reprice, relaunch. The second attempt — unplanned, tax-forced, but now live — began in April 2026.

We have spent time on the ground since. Dealer visits across Mumbai and Pune in May 2026 produced primary evidence that is, in places, surprising. What we found did not settle the question — but it sharpened it considerably. Royal Enfield showrooms are running three-month waiting periods on standard models. Triumph is delivering within forty-eight hours. The demand asymmetry is unmistakable. So is the product asymmetry — Triumph's salesperson is better trained, the warranty terms are genuinely superior, and the service anxiety that suppressed the first attempt's conversions is, at least in Mumbai, beginning to be addressed. But the Royal Enfield salesman who has driven his Classic 350 for nine years and says he will never touch another bike is also real evidence. So is the young Hunter buyer who cross-shopped Triumph and came back.

Our view, stated plainly: this is not the same contest as 2022. The pricing has changed materially. For a specific and identifiable buyer — young, urban, first premium motorcycle, Hunter 350 consideration set — Triumph is now a genuine lateral choice, not an aspirational stretch. That matters. But the outcome depends on a strategic decision Bajaj has not yet made publicly: whether to invest in Triumph's distribution at the scale and pace required to make this a national story rather than a metropolitan one. The product case is made. The distribution case is not yet funded. Watch Bajaj's dealer count, not Triumph's horsepower. The former determines everything.

## Investment Conclusion

We are constructive on Bajaj Auto and watchful on Eicher Motors.

Bajaj's two-wheeler premium franchise has more structural credibility today than at any point in the last decade. Triumph now competes at Royal Enfield's price point, with a globally recognised brand, on a fully amortised platform. The R&D is sunk. The manufacturing is running. The incremental margin on volume is real. The market is still pricing Bajaj's premium two-wheeler story as an experiment that has not proved itself. We think that characterisation is stale.

Eicher is a franchise that remains largely intact but is, for the first time, under genuine competitive pressure in a segment it has owned without contest. The Classic 350 and Bullet 350 are defended by loyalty that is deep enough to be irrational — and we mean that as a compliment. The Hunter 350 is a different case. It is Royal Enfield's most modern, fastest-growing model, designed for exactly the buyer Triumph is now targeting at price parity. Hunter contributes approximately 18-20% of Royal Enfield's total 350cc volumes. It is not the whole franchise. It is the margin of growth. And above 350cc, the GST wall that Eicher's premiumisation story must now climb is permanent and steep.

We maintain Market-Perform on Eicher with a downward bias that crystallises — or doesn't — at festive season FY27. We are constructive on Bajaj, with the size of our conviction tied directly to the pace of Triumph's distribution expansion over the next four quarters.

*The single variable that determines our next move on both stocks: Triumph India's dealer count outside the top ten cities by December 2026. That number tells us whether the second attempt is serious.*

## 1. Triumph in India — A Decade of Trying

To judge the second attempt fairly, the first attempt deserves a proper accounting. It has been more instructive than the headline volume numbers suggest.

Triumph Motorcycles arrived in India in 2013, initially as a completely built-up import — CBU units assembled in the UK and Thailand, landed at Indian ports, sold to a wealthy niche buyer through a handful of premium dealerships. The positioning was unambiguously aspirational: Bonneville T100s, Tigers, Thunderbirds, priced at ₹7-12 lakh. This was not a mass-market play. It was a brand-establishment play — building awareness and credibility among the upper segment of Indian riders before the volume market became addressable. Triumph India in this phase had perhaps thirty to forty dealerships and volumes that would have been rounding errors on any serious volume forecast.

The strategic inflection came in 2020, when Bajaj Auto and Triumph Motorcycles UK announced a partnership that changed the nature of the India story entirely. Bajaj would co-develop and manufacture a range of mid-sized Triumph motorcycles at its Chakan facility in Pune. The bikes would carry full Triumph branding and DNA — British styling, Triumph's engine character, genuine parent-company involvement in engineering — but would be built in India at Indian costs and sold at Indian prices. The first product of this partnership, the Speed 400, launched in July 2023 at ₹2.33 lakh. A scrambler variant followed. CBU imports of larger-displacement Triumphs — the Tiger 900, the Bonneville T120, the Rocket 3 — continued alongside the locally manufactured range, keeping the brand's aspirational ceiling intact.

**Exhibit 1: Triumph India — From CBU Import to Local Manufacture**

| Phase | Period | Model range | Manufacturing | Price range | Bajaj involvement | Strategic intent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CBU import era | 2013–2022 | Tiger, Bonneville T100/T120, Thunderbird, Street Twin | UK / Thailand CBU | ₹7–15 lakh | None | Brand establishment; premium niche |
| Partnership transition | 2020–2023 | Announcement; product co-development | Chakan facility built out | — | Design and manufacturing JV signed | Volume market entry in preparation |
| Local manufacture — Phase 1 | 2023–2025 | Speed 400 (398cc), Scrambler 400 X (398cc) | **Pune, Chakan — fully local** | ₹2.33–2.64 lakh | Full — engine, assembly, distribution | Aspirational upgrade from RE; 398cc positioning |
| Local manufacture — Phase 2 | April 2026 onwards | Speed T4, Speed 400, Scrambler 400 X, Bonneville 350 — all at **349cc** | **Pune, Chakan — fully local** | ₹1.85–2.60 lakh | Full | GST-forced repositioning; RE price-point entry |
| CBU continues (large displacement) | Ongoing | Tiger 900, Speed Triple, Rocket 3 | UK / Thailand CBU | ₹12–21 lakh | Distribution only | Aspirational ceiling; brand halo |

*Source: Company announcements, Triumph India press releases, Bernstein research.*

Three things about this history matter for the current analysis. First, the local manufacturing shift is complete and irreversible — the Chakan facility is running, the supply chain is established, and Triumph's 349cc bikes are as domestic as Royal Enfield's in terms of manufacturing economics. The spare parts anxiety that suppressed first-attempt conversions is being actively addressed; our dealer visits in May 2026 were explicitly told that parts manufactured in Pune are available across the service network without the lead times that plagued the CBU era. Second, the CBU halo — Tiger 900s and Rocket 3s in the same showroom as a ₹1.89 lakh Speed T4 — is a genuine brand asset. The aspirational ceiling makes the entry-level price feel like a bargain rather than a compromise. Royal Enfield cannot replicate that architecture. Third, and most importantly: the partnership gives Bajaj full commercial control over Triumph's India distribution, pricing, and expansion decisions. Whether Triumph's India dealer network grows from 150 to 400 outlets over the next three years is Bajaj's decision alone. That fact concentrates our analytical attention precisely where it belongs.

## 2. What GST 2.0 Actually Did — Forced Repositioning, Not Strategic Aggression

The tax change is widely understood as having created an opportunity for Bajaj. The more precise framing is that it created an obligation.

Before September 2025, motorcycles across the 300-400cc range attracted broadly similar GST rates — there was no meaningful fiscal cliff between 349cc and 398cc. Bajaj's above-350cc positioning was a free commercial choice. GST 2.0 made it expensive to maintain.

**Exhibit 2: The GST Shock — What Changed Overnight**

| Bracket | GST before (effective blended) | GST after | Change | ₹ impact on ₹2L motorcycle | ₹ impact on ₹3L motorcycle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≤350cc | ~28% | **18%** | −10pp | −₹17,000 (benefit) | −₹26,000 (benefit) |
| >350cc | ~28-31% | **40%** | +9-12pp | +₹18,000–24,000 (penalty) | +₹27,000–36,000 (penalty) |
| **Gap across 350cc line** | **~0-3pp** | **22pp** | **+19-22pp** | **₹35,000–41,000** | **₹53,000–62,000** |

*Source: Government of India GST Council notification, September 2025.*

With the full 22-point gap now operative, Bajaj's 398cc Triumph lineup was sitting at a structural price disadvantage that it had not budgeted for. The engineering response — boring the 398cc single to 349.13cc, retaining stroke and losing almost nothing in performance — was trivial in execution. The commercial unlock was significant. Six Triumph variants relaunched at 349cc in April 2026; the Pulsar NS400Z, Dominar 400, and KTM 390 Duke followed within weeks.

**Exhibit 3: The Repricing — What GST 2.0 Did to the Triumph vs. Royal Enfield Price Gap**

| Triumph model | Old price (₹L) at 398cc | New price (₹L) at 349cc | Price reduction | RE equivalent | RE price (₹L) | Old gap (Triumph vs RE) | **New gap (Triumph vs RE)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speed 400 | 2.33–2.40 | 2.15–2.25 | −₹15-18k | Classic 350 | 1.90–2.16 | +₹17–50k | **+₹0–35k** |
| Speed T4 | ~2.10 | 1.85–2.00 | −₹10-15k | Hunter 350 | 1.85–2.00 | +₹10–60k | **₹0–10k** |
| Scrambler 400 X | 2.64 | 2.45–2.60 | −₹10-15k | Himalayan 450 | 2.85–3.20 | −₹20-55k (Triumph cheaper) | −₹25–75k (Triumph cheaper) |
| Bonneville 350 | — (new) | ~2.40–2.60 (est.) | New entry | Classic 350 | 1.90–2.16 | n/a | **+₹25–45k** |

*Source: Company price lists, dealer quotes, Bernstein estimates. All prices ex-showroom Mumbai.*

The Speed T4 row tells the whole story in one line. A gap of ₹10,000-60,000 — wide enough that the Hunter buyer was simply not cross-shopping — has compressed to ₹0-10,000. That is not a smaller premium. It is price parity. For the first time, a buyer choosing between a Hunter 350 and a Triumph Speed T4 is making a lateral decision: same segment, similar price, two very different propositions. The nature of the competitive conversation has changed fundamentally.

## 3. In the Field — What We Found

*Dealer visits conducted across Mumbai and Pune, May 2026. Observations are first-hand.*

**The Royal Enfield studio.**

The first thing the salesperson mentions, before anything about the product, is the waiting period. Standard models: three months. The make-to-order programme — bespoke colour combinations, specific accessory configurations — carries a 45-60 day wait at a ₹5,000-6,000 premium over the standard list price. He mentions this not as an apology but as a credential. The waiting period is the demand signal, and the showroom knows it. A three-month queue is not a supply chain failure. It is evidence that the brand is operating at a level of demand its plant — running close to full capacity — cannot immediately absorb.

The store itself has been designed to feel like a destination rather than a showroom. Dark walls, amber lighting, the Classic 350 variants arranged with space between them. The feel is deliberate — cult, not retail. Every surface reinforces the same message: this is not a transaction, it is an initiation. The salesperson himself has driven a Classic 350 for nine years. He tells us this unprompted, and we believe him entirely. When asked about Triumph, he pauses — not out of concern, but out of the mild bemusement of someone being asked to compare their religion to another. "Triumph is for someone who wants performance," he says. "This is for someone who wants to feel something." He then walks our Hunter enquiry to the service window and names three Royal Enfield-authorised service points within five kilometres. The move is practised. He has done it many times.

Footfall is strong. Four customers in two hours — not extraordinary, but purposeful. The buyers walking in have largely decided. The test ride is a formality for the Classic and Bullet enquiries. The Hunter enquiry — a young man in his mid-twenties — is the exception. He sits on three variants, asks specific questions, and mentions Triumph twice. He leaves without buying.

**The Triumph showroom.**

The Triumph showroom is also dark-walled and amber-lit — and this is the first interesting observation. Both brands have independently converged on the same visual language: cult, premium, unhurried. The aesthetic competition is closer than the product competition. The Triumph salesperson is noticeably better trained — more fluent on specification, quicker with comparison data, more practiced at handling the "but what about Royal Enfield?" question. When we ask him directly how he thinks about the competition with Royal Enfield, the response is immediate and, in its confidence, telling: "Royal Enfield is not our competition. Look at what we offer versus what they offer." He says this without hesitation, with the assurance of someone who has been told this and believes it.

He is wrong in a narrow sense — the Hunter buyer is clearly cross-shopping — and right in a broader one. The Triumph Speed T4 and the Classic 350 are not competing for the same person. But the Hunter buyer and the Speed T4 buyer overlap, and the salesperson knows this; he mentions Hunter enquiries three times in our conversation, each time with quiet satisfaction.

Delivery is within forty-eight hours. No waiting period. The contrast with Royal Enfield's three-month queue is stark — though its interpretation is ambiguous. Forty-eight-hour delivery could mean either that supply is ample or that demand is modest. In this case, it is both. Triumph's Pune facility can produce significantly more than current demand requires.

On the service anxiety question — the suppressor of the first attempt's conversions — the salesperson is prepared. Since the move to local manufacture in Pune, spare parts are available across the service network without the six-to-eight-week lead times of the CBU era. Service centres are opening in Mumbai's extended suburbs. The ten-year warranty — against Royal Enfield's three years standard, with a five-year extension purchasable at ₹6,000 — is deployed as a closing argument. The service interval is sixteen thousand kilometres or one year, whichever is earlier; he claims this is more generous than any competitor in the segment.

Whether this is enough to overcome the service network asymmetry — Triumph's roughly 150 outlets against Royal Enfield's 2,000 — is the question the showroom cannot answer, because the answer does not exist yet. In Mumbai, the anxiety is largely addressed. In Nagpur or Lucknow or Coimbatore, there is no Triumph showroom to walk into in the first place.

**What the visits tell us.**

The cross-shopping is real and new. Royal Enfield's salespeople encounter the Triumph name in Hunter conversations regularly; twelve months ago they did not. Triumph's salespeople hear about Hunter enquiries with a frequency that confirms the overlap. But — and this is the critical observation — conversion from cross-shopping to purchase at Triumph is not yet visible in volume data. The demand at Royal Enfield is validated by a three-month queue and a plant at capacity. Triumph is delivering in forty-eight hours. The aspiration competition is closer than it has ever been. The transaction competition has not yet begun.

One observation from the Royal Enfield visit stays with us. Their salesperson mentioned, as a matter of course, that when a young buyer walks in with his parents, the parents frequently drive the decision toward Royal Enfield. Brand legacy, service network familiarity, the certainty of a known resale value — these are parental considerations, not aspirational ones. The top-down purchase dynamic in Royal Enfield's favour is real, and it operates precisely in the Hunter buyer demographic that Triumph is targeting. A twenty-two-year-old who wants a Triumph Speed T4 but whose parents are paying may end up with a Hunter 350 not because he chose it but because they did.

The thump, the posture, the feeling — our own test rides confirmed this is not romantic exaggeration. The Royal Enfield 350cc engine's character is distinctive in a way that is either irrelevant to you or entirely the reason you are there. For the Hunter buyer discovering it for the first time, that character may or may not resonate. Whether it does determines everything.

## 6. Eicher's Two-Sided Problem — Core Under Pressure, Premiumisation Stalled

The Hunter conversation is where the near-term debate concentrates. For the Eicher stock, however, the medium-term problem sits above 350cc — and it is structural in a way that brand strength alone cannot address.

The same GST ruling that brought Triumph to price parity at 349cc simultaneously taxed Royal Enfield's entire aspirational range — Guerrilla 450, Himalayan 450, 650 twins — at 40%. These are the products the stock's premium multiple is paying for: the gradual migration upmarket toward higher ASP, higher margins, a brand that competes at ₹3-5 lakh rather than ₹2 lakh.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Himalayan 450 | 452cc | 40% | 2.85–3.20 | Scrambler 400 X (349cc) | 18% | 2.45–2.60 | **+₹40,000–75,000** | High — both adventure-oriented |

For how many, and for how long, is the question the stock is asking.

Royal Enfield's share in the 350cc-and-above premium segment has already declined from 77% in FY22 to 46% in FY26 — a 31-point erosion over five years, before the full weight of GST 2.0 is felt. This is the earnings upgrade path the market is paying for. It is now the segment where the competitive disadvantage is greatest and most permanent.

Five of seven models are not competing for Royal Enfield's buyer. It is also the product launching without prior precedent in India: the first motorcycle to fight Royal Enfield on emotional ground rather than rational specification. Whether the Classic 350 buyer considers it seriously, we will know at Diwali.

## 8. The Distribution Imperative — What Separates Attempt Two from Attempt One

The first attempt failed for three reasons: price was wrong, product character was misaligned, and distribution was thin. GST 2.0 has largely addressed the first. Our field work suggests the second was always overstated for the Hunter buyer. The third — distribution — remains exactly as thin as it was, and it is the variable that determines whether the second attempt ends differently.

The table above maps precisely where the competitive risk to Royal Enfield is confined. In Tier-1 India, Triumph's service anxiety is manageable and the brand is known. The competition is real. In Tier-2 India — where Royal Enfield has been growing its Hunter and Classic volumes aggressively and which now accounts for an estimated 40-45% of total Royal Enfield sales — Triumph is effectively absent. The Hunter buyer in Nagpur or Coimbatore who cross-shops Triumph online and then asks "where do I get it serviced?" receives an answer that ends the conversation.

This geography is the single most important reason Royal Enfield's franchise has not been seriously tested by the first attempt. The competitive risk is, today, almost entirely confined to the top eight to ten cities. It is real in those cities. It is contained everywhere else.

## 10. The Verdict

The first attempt failed because the price was wrong. The product was not the problem then, and it is not now. GST 2.0 fixed the price for one specific model — the Speed T4 versus the Hunter 350 — and narrowed it materially across the broader lineup. That change is real, permanent, and has already produced the first observable competitive signal: Royal Enfield's salespeople are hearing the Triumph name in Hunter conversations in a way they were not twelve months ago.

The second attempt will succeed — genuinely, materially, in a way that moves Eicher's earnings and re-rates Bajaj's franchise — only if Bajaj commits to distribution investment at a scale it has not yet publicly signalled. Fifteen years of Royal Enfield's franchise dominance was built on brand, on community, on resale certainty. But it was also built on being present — two thousand dealerships, four thousand service points, a mechanic within ten kilometres of wherever you live in India. Triumph at 150 outlets can threaten Royal Enfield in Bandra and Koramangala. It cannot threaten it in Nashik or Coimbatore or Bhopal. Not yet.

The Classic 350 and Bullet 350 are, in our assessment, structurally defended. The loyalty our dealer visits documented — the nine-year Classic 350 salesman, the top-down parental purchase dynamic, the cult that Royal Enfield has built over decades — is not a marketing claim. It is observable behaviour. The Bonneville 350 is the first emotional test of Classic loyalty and it deserves to be taken seriously. But it arrives at a premium, through thin distribution, against a brand that has had the field entirely to itself for two decades. The burden of proof is on Triumph.

The Hunter 350 is a different case — genuinely contested, for the first time, by a competitor at price parity. The Hunter buyer is younger, more persuadable, less anchored by the character preference that makes the Classic buyer immoveable. He is the buyer Royal Enfield needs most for its long-term franchise health. He is also the buyer most likely to walk out of the Triumph showroom with the keys.

Eighteen to twenty percent of Royal Enfield's total 350cc volumes are Hunter. Above 350cc, the Guerrilla 450 faces a ₹70,000-plus structural price disadvantage against its nearest Triumph rival. Both of these represent real and present headwinds for a stock priced for a premiumisation journey that the tax structure has simultaneously incentivised below 350cc and penalised above it.

We are not sellers of Eicher today. The franchise is too strong and the evidence too early. We are, however, no longer comfortable buyers. Market-Perform, with the direction of our next move determined by one number: Triumph's dealer count outside the top ten cities by December 2026.

On Bajaj: the optionality is real and underpriced. Whether Bajaj chooses to exercise it is the question. We think it will. The infrastructure is built, the platform is amortised, the product is validated, and the price condition that defeated the first attempt has been resolved by the tax code. The rational course is to back this aggressively. We are constructive, and we become more so with every Triumph outlet that opens in a city where Royal Enfield currently competes uncontested.

**The drawbridge came down by accident. Whether someone crosses it is a choice.**
